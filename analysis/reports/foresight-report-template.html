What a machine learning model can and cannot tell you about Bitcoin

It cannot tell you which way the price is going. It can tell you how violent the next few hours will be — and the further ahead you look, the better it gets. Here is the evidence, including where it fails.

The question everyone asks first

And the answer nobody wants

Bitcoin's next-minute direction is not predictable. Not with gradient boosting, not with a neural network, not with momentum. Using the previous minute's return as a forecast scores an R² of −1.02 against simply predicting zero, with a hit rate of 49.4% — below a coin flip.

That is not a modelling failure, it is a property of the market. The lag-1 autocorrelation of minute returns is statistically significant across 3.3 million observations, but it is negative — mean reversion consistent with bid–ask bounce. It lives inside the spread, so it cannot be traded.

What is predictable

Volatility, and strongly

The direction of the next move is noise. Its size is not. Absolute returns autocorrelate at +0.42 at one minute and remain at +0.25 a full hour later. Big moves cluster; calm clusters too.

The finding worth sharing

The advantage grows with the horizon

Against HAR — Corsi's heterogeneous autoregressive model, the standard benchmark in the realized-volatility literature — gradient boosting improves accuracy by 18.7% at one hour. At four hours it reaches 30.1%, and holds 28.0% at a full day.

This runs against the usual intuition that forecasts decay with horizon. What decays is the benchmark: HAR's fixed hourly/daily/weekly structure is tuned for one step ahead, while the model can exploit slower-moving state that only pays off further out. The horizons that turn out to be easier are also the ones an operator actually cares about.

All three are significant beyond any conventional threshold under a Diebold–Mariano test with Newey–West errors, which is the right test here because forecast errors arrive in runs rather than independently.

Where the edge comes from

One feature the benchmark structurally cannot have

Half the model's explanatory power sits on the previous hour and a fifth on the previous day — that is HAR's own structure, recovered from data. The third most important feature, at 13.5% of total gain, is downside semivariance: volatility computed from negative minutes only.

This is the leverage effect. A 1% fall raises expected future volatility more than a 1% rise does. HAR's realized-variance term is symmetric by construction and has no way to express it. That asymmetry is most of the improvement, which is why a richer model wins rather than simply a bigger one.

Where it fails

Stated plainly, because this is the part that matters

The model does not predict crises. In the most violent hour of the evaluation window, realized volatility reached 6.65%. The median forecast said 1.07%; the top of the 90% band said 1.73%; HAR said 0.68%.

The model is closer than the benchmark, and its band does widen by 2.7× ahead of extreme hours, so it is not blind. But being a factor of four short is the difference between a useful risk signal and a sufficient one.

Honest accounting

An earlier round of this work read the held-out test window twice — once before a feature defect was found, once after. The defect was an unbounded ratio that reached 9.3 in training and 1,929 in test, which made the linear and neural models diverge catastrophically. It was fixed on grounds independent of test performance, but that split is no longer pristine. Every headline number here comes from walk-forward evaluation instead, which does not depend on it.

The negative result worth as much as the positive one

A neural network was included as a control. It beat the benchmark on validation by 6.7% and then lost to it on the test window by 4.2%. Gradient boosting held its edge on both.

Without a held-out evaluation the neural network would have looked like it worked. On tabular financial data with a few dozen engineered features, it does not — and that is worth knowing before anyone spends a month on architecture.

How it was kept honest

  • Walk-forward evaluation. Expanding window, refit every 90 days, every block scored before it is ever trained on.
  • Chronological splits only. Never a random split of a time series; scalers fitted on training windows alone.
  • Gap-aware returns. A return spanning a 33-hour exchange outage is not a one-minute return, and is dropped rather than kept.
  • Every source file checksum-verified against the SHA-256 published beside it — 108 monthly archives, none unverified.
  • A real benchmark. HAR is not a straw man: it beats the naive carry-forward by 9%, and beats both smoothers tested.

One data quirk is worth repeating because it nearly cost a fifth of the sample. The loader reported a 14-day exchange outage in December 2017 — during the peak of the mania, which is implausible on its face. It turned out to be 20,400 real bars whose timestamps sat a few seconds into the minute and so fell outside the expected grid. The implausibility was the only thing that flagged it.
